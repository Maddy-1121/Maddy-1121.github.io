How I contribute across a project

From first drawing to final handover.

Construction projects are not a collection of disconnected tasks. Each phase changes the information, cost, schedule, and decisions in the next. This is how my project engineering responsibilities fit into a typical US general contractor workflow. The phase sequence is a representative framework; my involvement and responsibility level have varied by project and employer.

01
Opportunity & preconstruction

Understand the project before pricing it

Work starts with drawings, specifications, scope narratives, milestones and constraints. I review available information to identify what is included, what is missing, and where scope boundaries need clarification before teams commit to a price or delivery plan.

  • Review architectural, structural and MEP drawings and specifications to understand work packages.
  • Break scope into trade-level quantities and identify assumptions, exclusions and potential design gaps.
  • Support RFPs, RFQs, bid documentation, scope sheets and subcontractor questions.
  • Organize quantity takeoffs and bid comparison information for estimator and PM review.

Typical outputs: Quantity takeoff workbook · Bid scope checklist · RFQ package · Clarification log · Bid comparison matrix

02
Design development & coordination

Make drawings usable for construction

Design information evolves. A project engineer needs to recognize which revisions affect scope, constructability, cost and downstream work. I coordinate between disciplines, track design changes and support resolution of conflicts before they become field problems.

  • Overlay drawing revisions and document changes affecting material quantities and installation sequences.
  • Review Revit/Navisworks models for coordination issues across architectural, structural and MEP systems.
  • Prepare and track RFIs, support issue discussions, and maintain traceability between design questions and responses.
  • Review submittals against drawing/specification requirements and flag mismatches for the responsible reviewer.

Typical outputs: Revision tracker · Coordination issue log · RFI register · Submittal review comments · Model clash reports

03
Planning & mobilization

Turn scope into an executable plan

Once the work is defined, it needs a schedule, procurement sequence, responsible parties and decision dates. My role is to help project leadership keep these pieces connected, particularly where long-lead equipment, approvals or occupied-site constraints affect progress.

  • Support baseline schedule development, activity sequencing, milestones and look-ahead planning.
  • Identify information or procurement dependencies that may affect installation readiness.
  • Prepare coordination agendas and action-item logs for project teams and trade partners.
  • Track required approvals, shop drawings and purchasing milestones against upcoming work.

Typical outputs: Look-ahead schedule · Action-item register · Procurement log · Milestone tracker · Responsibility matrix

04
Procurement & buyout

Keep scope, vendors and lead times aligned

Procurement is not only placing orders. It means confirming the correct scope, clarifying qualifications, evaluating responses and tracking decisions before they affect field execution.

  • Assist with vendor outreach, RFQs, scope comparisons and bid leveling.
  • Track submittal approvals, material requirements, purchase orders and delivery commitments.
  • Document open procurement decisions and communicate their schedule implications.
  • Coordinate with PMs and trade partners on exclusions, alternates and scope changes.

Typical outputs: Bid leveling sheet · Vendor comparison · Procurement register · PO support documents · Long-lead tracker

05
Construction execution

Keep decisions moving while work progresses

During construction, project information changes quickly. I support the communication and documentation workflows that help field teams work from current information and escalate issues before they create avoidable rework.

  • Coordinate drawing clarifications, RFIs, submittals and open design issues.
  • Prepare or maintain meeting minutes, action logs and coordination follow-ups.
  • Support subcontractor communication and resolve documentation gaps through the responsible team.
  • Track constraints, look-ahead activities and changes affecting planned work.

Typical outputs: OAC minutes · Updated RFI/submittal logs · Constraint register · Coordination reports · Look-ahead updates

06
Cost, change & reporting

Make project performance visible

Scope changes, invoice reviews and reporting need to be traceable to actual project information. I help assemble and reconcile the documents used by project managers to evaluate impacts and communicate status.

  • Track potential changes, design revisions, quotations and related supporting documentation.
  • Assist with invoice, procurement and financial reporting workflows.
  • Build structured Excel and dashboard trackers to consolidate project data and highlight open actions.
  • Support schedule-impact and scope-impact discussions by keeping source records organized.

Typical outputs: Change log · Cost comparison · Invoice tracker · Project dashboard · Weekly status report

07
Quality, turnover & closeout

Finish with a usable record—not just completed work

Project delivery requires evidence of what was installed, approved and handed over. My experience includes supporting closeout documentation and tracking outstanding issues through completion.

  • Organize punch list items, review comments and responsible-party follow-up.
  • Track closeout submittals and turnover requirements.
  • Support the assembly of final records and documentation packages.
  • Help teams identify missing deliverables before handover.

Typical outputs: Punch list tracker · Closeout checklist · Turnover document index · Outstanding-items log

Case study A · Large-scale US education project

Managing MEP quantities through design revisions

ContextDesign-build education building
RoleProject engineering / coordination
ToolsRevit, Navisworks, Bluebeam, BIM 360

The project challenge: Mechanical, plumbing and related MEP systems were being developed and revised while project teams needed reliable quantities and coordinated drawings. A takeoff completed once would quickly become outdated if revisions were not systematically reviewed.

  1. Establish the scope: Review model and drawing information, identify relevant ductwork, piping and medical-gas scope, and organize measurable categories.
  2. Build the takeoff: Quantify relevant systems and document assumptions so the quantities can be reviewed and updated.
  3. Control revisions: Compare updated information against earlier design versions, track affected elements and assess quantity implications.
  4. Resolve coordination issues: Review interdisciplinary clashes and drawing discrepancies, prepare questions and track clarifications with project teams.
  5. Support downstream delivery: Maintain issue records, contribute to RFI/submittal review and communicate updated information for project decisions.

Value to a GC: clearer quantity history, fewer unresolved design questions and better traceability between revisions and project decisions.

Specific project-level savings and outcomes are intentionally not claimed here without supporting, shareable records.

ContextHealthcare and commercial projects
RoleSenior Project Engineer
FocusCoordination, procurement, controls

The project challenge: On active projects, decisions are distributed across owners, architects, contractors and vendors. RFIs, bids, procurement, schedule constraints, change documentation and invoices must remain consistent even as priorities change.

  3. Move procurement forward: Prepare bid packages, compare scope and quotations, and track vendor information.
  4. Maintain project controls: Organize schedule, financial and change records into structured logs and reporting workflows.
  5. Close the loop: Escalate unresolved items, update documentation and provide the PM with an actionable project picture.

Value to a GC: fewer information gaps between teams, clearer accountability and more dependable project reporting.

Case study C · Cross-project technical workflow

From a drawing discrepancy to a documented resolution

This example illustrates a typical workflow used in project engineering, not a claim about a particular named RFI.

  1. Detect: Compare plans, specifications and model information and identify a conflicting or missing detail.
  2. Verify: Check related views, notes and revisions to avoid issuing a question already answered elsewhere.
  3. Document: Record the exact location, drawing references, proposed clarification and potential scope or schedule effect.
  4. Coordinate: Route the issue through the appropriate RFI or coordination channel and follow up with stakeholders.
  5. Implement: Link the response to updated drawings, takeoff assumptions, submittals or change documentation as applicable.

My approach: the value of an RFI is not the number issued—it is whether the project team reaches a clear, timely, traceable decision.
